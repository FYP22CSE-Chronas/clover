\title{Learned Marginals in a Coherent Hierarchical Forecaster:\\
Where a Conditional Normalizing Flow Helps, and Where It Does Not}

\begin{abstract}
CLOVER produces coherent probabilistic forecasts for hierarchical time series by
sampling a Gaussian factor model at the bottom level and aggregating every draw
through the summation matrix, so that coherence holds by construction and the
scoring rule can be optimised directly. The construction fixes the bottom-level
marginal to be Gaussian. We replace that fixed marginal with a learned one: a
conditional deep sigmoidal flow whose weights, slopes and shifts are emitted per
series and per horizon by the same head that emits the location, scale and factor
loadings, leaving the encoder, mixer, decoder, aggregation step and training
objective untouched. We also evaluate a second, copula-correct placement of the
same idea, in which a monotonic rational-quadratic spline warps the entire
correlated draw rather than only its idiosyncratic component, so that rank
correlation is provably preserved. On seven public hierarchies with twelve
matched seeds per configuration, the flow head lowers overall scaled CRPS on five
of six datasets, significantly at the $5\%$ level on three of them (labour
$-1.8\%$, prison $-7.3\%$, tourism-small $-6.6\%$; paired Wilcoxon), and raises
it on traffic by $5.9\%$. The spline head lowers it on four of seven,
significantly on two, and its wins are on a largely disjoint set of datasets from
the flow's. We then test the motivation itself. Using seasonal-naive
standardised residuals, an omnibus normality test rejects the Gaussian
hypothesis for between $10.7\%$ and $100\%$ of bottom-level series depending on
the dataset, with excess kurtosis rather than skewness the dominant departure.
The association between that measured departure and the flow's benefit is
positive (Spearman $\rho=+0.89$, $p=0.019$, $n=6$ datasets) --- that is, the
learned marginal helps \emph{least} where the Gaussian assumption is most
violated, the opposite of the motivating hypothesis. We show that the
implementation explains part of this: because the flow warps only the
idiosyncratic component and a Gaussian factor term is added afterwards, the
bottom-level marginal is a convolution of a warped Gaussian with a Gaussian, and
the non-Gaussian shape is progressively undone as the factor term's variance
share grows. We report the result as mixed and dataset-dependent, identify the
confounds that seven datasets cannot separate, and list the experiments needed
before the finding can be called conclusive.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Hierarchical time series arise wherever a quantity is reported at several levels of
aggregation at once: tourism by country, state and region; retail sales by item,
store and chain; traffic by lane, carriageway and road. Forecasts of such a
collection are \emph{coherent} when the forecast of every aggregate equals the sum
of the forecasts of its components. Coherence is an operational requirement, not an
aesthetic one: a plan built on incoherent forecasts allocates resources that do not
add up.

Classical work achieves coherence by \emph{reconciliation} --- forecast each series
independently, then project the vector of base forecasts onto the coherent subspace
\citep{hyndman2011optimal,wickramasuriya2019optimal}. Probabilistic reconciliation
extends this to distributions \citep{taieb2017coherent,panagiotelis2023probabilistic},
and a line of neural work instead makes coherence structural, so that no projection
step is needed \citep{rangapuram2021endtoend,olivares2024clover}.

CLOVER \citep{olivares2024clover} belongs to the second group. It predicts, for
each bottom-level series and each horizon step, a location $\mu$, a scale $\sigma$
and a vector of $K$ factor loadings, then draws samples by reparameterisation,
\begin{equation}
\bvec = \mu + \sigma \odot z + \Fmat\,\varepsilon ,
\qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_K),
\label{eq:clover-sample}
\end{equation}
and aggregates each draw through the fixed summation matrix. Every draw is
therefore coherent, and because the draw is differentiable in the parameters, the
scoring rule can be minimised on the aggregated distribution directly rather than
reconciled afterwards.

Equation~\eqref{eq:clover-sample} makes the bottom-level predictive distribution
Gaussian --- exactly Gaussian, with a covariance that is diagonal plus rank $K$.
That is a strong assumption about the shape of a predictive distribution, and the
literature on non-hierarchical probabilistic forecasting has largely moved away
from it, through copulas with learned marginals \citep{salinas2019copula} and
through normalizing flows \citep{rasul2021multivariate}. The natural question is
whether the same move pays inside a coherent-by-construction hierarchical model,
where the Gaussian assumption is doing structural work: it is what makes the
factor term a clean, low-rank description of cross-series dependence.

\paragraph{Research question.} Can replacing CLOVER's fixed Gaussian marginal with
a learned conditional normalizing flow improve probabilistic forecasting accuracy
for hierarchical time series, and is any improvement explained by the degree to
which the data departs from normality?

\paragraph{Contributions.} We do not claim that flows for hierarchical forecasting
are new; Section~\ref{sec:related} documents at least three prior systems that
combine the two. What we contribute is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{controlled} substitution. We replace only the distribution head of a
coherent-by-construction factor model and hold the encoder, cross-series mixer,
decoder, exogenous features, optimiser, schedule, split, seeds and scoring
procedure fixed, so that the measured difference is attributable to the marginal
family and nothing else (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item A distinction between two \emph{placements} of a learned marginal inside
Equation~\eqref{eq:clover-sample} --- warping the idiosyncratic component $z$
alone, versus warping the whole correlated draw $z + \Fmat\varepsilon$ --- with a
proof-level argument that only the second preserves rank correlation, and evidence
that the two win on largely disjoint datasets (Sections~\ref{sec:method}
and~\ref{sec:results}).
\item A structural analysis showing that the first placement partially undoes its
own effect, because an additive Gaussian factor term convolves the warped marginal
back toward a Gaussian at a rate set by the factor term's variance share
(Section~\ref{sec:convolution}).
\item A measurement of the non-Gaussianity that motivates the method, and the
negative finding that it does not predict where the method helps
(Section~\ref{sec:nongauss}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Hierarchical time series}
Let the hierarchy have $\Nb$ bottom-level series and $\Na$ aggregates. Stack them as
$\yvec_t \in \Real^{\Na+\Nb}$ and let $\bvec_t \in \Real^{\Nb}$ hold the bottom level.
A summation matrix $\Smat \in \Real^{(\Na+\Nb)\times \Nb}$ encodes the structure, and
in this implementation is always in the form $\Smat = [\mathbf{A}; I_{\Nb}]$, so that
\begin{equation}
\yvec_t = \Smat\,\bvec_t .
\end{equation}
A forecast distribution is coherent when it is supported on $\{\Smat \bvec : \bvec \in
\Real^{\Nb}\}$.

\subsection{Probabilistic forecasting and the scoring rule}
We evaluate with the continuous ranked probability score, a strictly proper scoring
rule for a univariate predictive distribution \citep{gneiting2007strictly}. Given a
predictive sample $\{x^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}$ and an observation $y$, the unbiased
$U$-statistic estimator used for training is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{CRPS}}\bigl(y, \{x^{(n)}\}\bigr)
= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigl|x^{(n)} - y\bigr|
- \frac{1}{2N(N-1)}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\bigl|x^{(n)} - x^{(m)}\bigr| ,
\label{eq:crps-mc}
\end{equation}
which the implementation computes in $O(N \log N)$ via the sorted identity rather than
by forming the pairwise matrix \citep{zamo2018crps}. For reporting we use the
quantile-integral estimator on a $1\%$ grid, twice the mean pinball loss over
$q \in \{0.01,\dots,0.99\}$; the two estimators differ, and the repository's tests
pin the difference.

Following \citet{olivares2024clover} we report \emph{scaled} CRPS per aggregation
level $\ell$: the level's total CRPS divided by its total absolute target mass,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sCRPS}(\ell)
= \frac{\sum_{i \in \ell}\sum_{\tau=1}^{h}\mathrm{CRPS}_{i,\tau}}
       {\sum_{i \in \ell}\sum_{\tau=1}^{h}\bigl|y_{i,\tau}\bigr|} .
\label{eq:scrps}
\end{equation}
This is a ratio of sums, not a mean of ratios, so a level dominated by one large
series is dominated by that series' CRPS too. ``Overall'' applies
Equation~\eqref{eq:scrps} with $\ell$ the whole hierarchy.

\subsection{Normalizing flows}
A normalizing flow expresses a density as a diffeomorphic push-forward of a simple
base density \citep{rezende2015nf,papamakarios2021nf}. If $z \sim p_Z$ and
$x = T_\phi(z)$ with $T_\phi$ invertible and differentiable, then
\begin{equation}
\log p_X(x) = \log p_Z\bigl(T^{-1}_\phi(x)\bigr)
+ \log\Bigl|\det \frac{\partial T^{-1}_\phi(x)}{\partial x}\Bigr| .
\label{eq:cov}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cov} is the standard route to \emph{maximum-likelihood} training
of a flow. We state it here only to make clear that \textbf{our method does not use
it}: the objective is Equation~\eqref{eq:crps-mc}, a scoring rule on samples, so the
model is used purely as a differentiable simulator. Only the forward direction
$z \mapsto T_\phi(z)$ is ever evaluated, and neither the inverse nor the
log-determinant is implemented. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses what this
costs.

For the transform we use the \emph{deep sigmoidal flow} (DSF) of
\citet{huang2018naf}, a convex combination of logistic sigmoids composed with a
logit link, and for the second placement the monotonic rational-quadratic spline of
\citet{durkan2019nsf}.

\section{Problem formulation}

Fix a forecast creation time $t$. The model sees the bottom-level history
$Y = \bvec_{t-L:t} \in \Real^{L \times \Nb}$ together with static and known-future
covariates, and must emit a joint predictive distribution over the full hierarchy
$\yvec_{t:t+h} \in \Real^{h \times (\Na+\Nb)}$ that is (i) coherent and (ii) scores
well under Equation~\eqref{eq:scrps}.

Write $q_\theta$ for the bottom-level predictive law induced by the model's
parameters $\theta$. CLOVER restricts $q_\theta$ to the Gaussian family
$\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mu, \mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2) + \Fmat\Fmat^{\!\top}\bigr)$ at each
horizon step. Our question is what happens when the \emph{marginal} of $q_\theta$ is
allowed to leave that family while the low-rank dependence structure and the
coherence mechanism are retained. We are careful throughout to distinguish:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \textbf{marginal} law of one bottom series at one horizon step;
\item the \textbf{joint} law over the $\Nb$ bottom series at one horizon step, whose
dependence is what the factor loadings encode;
\item \textbf{coherence}, a support constraint on the aggregated law, which in this
architecture is a property of each individual draw and therefore holds for any
marginal family.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The baseline, as implemented}
We describe the architecture as it exists in the repository, which is the object our
experiments actually vary.

\paragraph{Per-window normalisation.} From the insample block only, a location and
scale are computed per series, $\ell_j = \mathrm{mean}_t Y_{tj}$ and
$s_j = \mathrm{std}_t Y_{tj} + \epsilon$, and the window is standardised,
$\tilde{Y} = (Y - \ell)/s$. The horizon is never used to compute them. Two of the
eight configurations use a scale-only variant for sparse count data.

\paragraph{Encoder.} The standardised history is lifted to every hierarchy row,
$\tilde{Y}^{\mathrm{hier}} = \Smat \tilde{Y}^{\!\top} \in \Real^{(\Na+\Nb) \times L}$,
and passed through a left-padded dilated causal convolution stack with weights shared
across rows, returning the last step: $H^{\mathrm{enc}} \in \Real^{(\Na+\Nb)\times C}$.
Aggregate rows are encoded, not just bottom rows.

\paragraph{Cross-series mixer.} Because $\Smat = [\mathbf{A}; I]$, the trailing $\Nb$
rows of $H^{\mathrm{enc}}$ are the bottom series themselves, and the mixer is residual
about them:
\begin{equation}
h^{\mathrm{hist}} = H^{\mathrm{enc}}_{[-\Nb:]}
+ \mathrm{MLP}\bigl(\mathrm{vec}(H^{\mathrm{enc}})\bigr)
\in \Real^{\Nb \times C}.
\end{equation}
Setting the mixer's hidden width to zero disables the second term; one of the seven
configurations (labour) does so.

\paragraph{Decoder.} Static and future covariate embeddings are concatenated onto
$h^{\mathrm{hist}}$ to form a context $c_i$, and a two-stage decoder produces one
feature vector per horizon step by concatenating a horizon-agnostic projection, a
horizon-specific projection and the raw future covariates of that step:
$z_{i,\tau} \in \Real^{D}$.

\paragraph{Head.} A multilayer perceptron maps $z_{i,\tau}$ to the distribution
parameters. For the Gaussian baseline it emits $2+K$ values,
\begin{equation}
\bigl(\mu_{i,\tau},\; \tilde\sigma_{i,\tau},\; \Fmat_{i,\tau,1:K}\bigr)
= \mathrm{MLP}(z_{i,\tau}),
\qquad
\sigma_{i,\tau} = \mathrm{softplus}(\tilde\sigma_{i,\tau}) + \epsilon_\sigma .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Denormalisation.} Because the factor model is affine in
$(\mu, \sigma, \Fmat)$, the per-window standardisation is inverted exactly on the
parameters rather than on the samples:
$\mu \leftarrow \mu s + \ell$, $\sigma \leftarrow \sigma s$,
$\Fmat \leftarrow \Fmat s$.

\paragraph{Sampling and coherence.} Draws are taken as in
Equation~\eqref{eq:clover-sample}, with $z_{i,\tau}^{(n)}$ independent across series
and $\varepsilon^{(n)}_{\tau} \in \Real^{K}$ shared across series, so that
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Cov}\bigl[\bvec_{\cdot,\tau}\bigr]
= \mathrm{diag}(\sigma^{2}_{\cdot,\tau}) + \Fmat_{\cdot,\tau}\Fmat_{\cdot,\tau}^{\!\top} .
\label{eq:cov-structure}
\end{equation}
Each draw is then clipped and aggregated, in that order:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\yvec}^{(n)}_{\tau} = \Smat \,\max\bigl(\bvec^{(n)}_{\tau}, 0\bigr).
\label{eq:coherent}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:coherent} is the entire coherence mechanism, and it is applied
identically for every head, so \emph{coherence is invariant to the choice of
marginal family}. Clipping before aggregating, rather than after, is what keeps the
aggregate equal to the sum of the values actually attributed to its children.

\paragraph{Objective.} The loss is Equation~\eqref{eq:crps-mc} applied to the
aggregated samples against the aggregated targets, summed over hierarchy rows and
horizon steps, divided by the batch's total absolute target mass --- a minibatch
surrogate for Equation~\eqref{eq:scrps}. Training uses $N=50$ Monte Carlo samples,
validation up to $500$, and test $1000$.

\subsection{Our modification: a conditional deep sigmoidal marginal}

We replace the Gaussian innovation $z$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:clover-sample} with a
learned monotone warp of it. The head is widened to emit $2 + 3K_f + K$ values,
adding a per-$(i,\tau)$ triple of raw weights, slopes and shifts, activated as
\begin{equation}
w = \mathrm{softmax}(\tilde{w}) \in \Delta^{K_f-1},
\qquad
a = \mathrm{softplus}(\tilde{a}) + a_{\min},
\qquad
b = \tilde{b},
\end{equation}
so that $w$ is a convex combination and every slope is bounded away from zero.
The transform is the deep sigmoidal flow \citep{huang2018naf}
\begin{equation}
u(z) \;=\; \mathrm{clip}\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_f} w_k\,
\varsigma\bigl(a_k z + b_k\bigr),\;\eta,\;1-\eta\Bigr),
\qquad
T_\phi(z) \;=\; \log u(z) - \log\bigl(1 - u(z)\bigr),
\label{eq:dsf}
\end{equation}
with $\varsigma$ the logistic sigmoid and $\eta = 10^{-6}$ the numerical guard that
keeps the logit finite. Samples become
\begin{equation}
\bvec^{(n)} = \mu + \sigma \odot T_\phi\bigl(z^{(n)}\bigr) + \Fmat\,\varepsilon^{(n)} .
\label{eq:flow-sample}
\end{equation}
The flow parameters are dimensionless and pass through denormalisation unchanged.

\paragraph{The flow is conditional.} $\phi = (w, a, b)$ is a function of
$z_{i,\tau}$, the decoder output, hence of the history, the covariates and the
hierarchy position. It is conditional in the sense that its parameters are amortised
by the network; it is not a flow that takes a conditioning vector as an input
argument.

\paragraph{Monotonicity and invertibility.} Differentiating
Equation~\eqref{eq:dsf},
\begin{equation}
T'_\phi(z) = \frac{1}{u(1-u)}\sum_{k=1}^{K_f} w_k\, a_k\,
\varsigma\bigl(a_k z + b_k\bigr)\bigl(1 - \varsigma(a_k z + b_k)\bigr) > 0 ,
\end{equation}
since $w_k > 0$ and $a_k \ge a_{\min} > 0$. So $T_\phi$ is strictly increasing and
invertible, though the inverse is never computed.

\paragraph{The Gaussian is nested, and is the initialisation.} If all units share a
slope and shift, $a_k \equiv a$ and $b_k \equiv b$, then
$\sum_k w_k \varsigma(az+b) = \varsigma(az+b)$ and therefore
\begin{equation}
T_\phi(z) = a z + b ,
\label{eq:nesting}
\end{equation}
exactly. The Gaussian marginal is thus a point of the flow's parameter space, not a
limit of it. In particular, at the implementation's initialisation
($\tilde{w} = \tilde{a} = \tilde{b} = 0$, $a_{\min} = 0.1$) we have
$a = \mathrm{softplus}(0) + 0.1 = 0.7931$ and $b = 0$, so
$T_\phi(z) = 0.7931\,z$: the flow head \emph{begins training as the Gaussian
baseline with a rescaled $\sigma$}. We verified both statements numerically to
within $10^{-6}$ over $z \in [-3,3]$. This matters for the fairness of the
comparison --- the flow head does not start from a handicapped initialisation and
spend training recovering the baseline.

\paragraph{An identifiability caveat.} $\Exp[T_\phi(z)] \ne 0$ and
$\mathrm{Var}[T_\phi(z)] \ne 1$ in general (for a randomly drawn $\phi$ we measured
$\Exp[T_\phi(z)] = -0.110$ and $\mathrm{sd} = 0.531$). Once the flow is non-affine,
$\mu$ is therefore no longer the predictive mean and $\sigma$ no longer its standard
deviation: the flow absorbs part of the location and scale. Nothing in the objective
is harmed, since CRPS sees only samples, but the parameters lose their
interpretation and the parameterisation is redundant.

\subsection{A second placement: warping the whole correlated draw}

Equation~\eqref{eq:flow-sample} warps the idiosyncratic component and leaves the
factor term Gaussian. The alternative is to build the correlated Gaussian first,
standardise it, and warp \emph{it}:
\begin{equation}
g = z + \Fmat \varepsilon,
\qquad
\varrho_i = \sqrt{1 + \|\Fmat_i\|^2},
\qquad
\bvec = \mu + \sigma \odot R_\psi\!\left(\frac{g}{\varrho}\right),
\label{eq:copula-sample}
\end{equation}
where $R_\psi$ is the monotonic rational-quadratic spline of \citet{durkan2019nsf},
with softmax-normalised bin widths and heights inside $[-\text{tb}, \text{tb}]$
($\text{tb}=4$), positive interior knot derivatives, boundary derivatives pinned to
one, and identity tails outside the bound. Because a strictly increasing elementwise
map leaves every rank statistic unchanged, Equation~\eqref{eq:copula-sample} alters
each marginal while preserving the \emph{rank} correlation induced by
Equation~\eqref{eq:cov-structure} exactly. This is a Gaussian copula in the sense of
\citet{sklar1959}, and is the same construction as
\citet{salinas2019copula} with a learned parametric marginal in place of an empirical
one. A third head composes the spline with a DSF layer; a composition of strictly
increasing maps is strictly increasing, so the guarantee survives.

We treat Equation~\eqref{eq:flow-sample} as the primary proposal, because it is the
minimal change to the marginal, and Equation~\eqref{eq:copula-sample} as the
principled alternative whose comparison isolates the effect of \emph{placement}.

\subsection{Why the first placement partially undoes itself}
\label{sec:convolution}

In Equation~\eqref{eq:flow-sample}, $\sigma_i T_\phi(z_i)$ and
$(\Fmat\varepsilon)_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \|\Fmat_i\|^2)$ are independent. The
marginal density of series $i$ is therefore a convolution,
\begin{equation}
p(\bvec_i) = \int
\varphi\bigl(v;\,0,\,\|\Fmat_i\|^2\bigr)\;
p_{\sigma_i T_\phi(z)}\bigl(\bvec_i - \mu_i - v\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}v ,
\label{eq:convolution}
\end{equation}
with $\varphi$ the Gaussian density. Gaussian smoothing shrinks every standardised
cumulant of order $\ge 3$. Define the Gaussian variance share
\begin{equation}
\rho_i \;=\;
\frac{\|\Fmat_i\|^2}{\sigma_i^2\,\mathrm{Var}[T_\phi(z)] + \|\Fmat_i\|^2}
\;\in\; [0,1] .
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
As $\rho_i \to 1$ the marginal tends to a Gaussian \emph{no matter what the flow
learns}; the flow's expressive power is spent on a component whose contribution to
the marginal is vanishing. Equation~\eqref{eq:copula-sample} has no such term, which
is the structural reason to expect the two placements to behave differently. We
regard this as the most plausible mechanism behind the result in
Section~\ref{sec:nongauss}, but note that $\rho_i$ is a property of a \emph{trained}
model and we did not measure it --- see Section~\ref{sec:missing}.

\paragraph{A limitation of the protocol, stated up front.} In every configuration the
test set is a \emph{single} forecast origin ($\text{test}=1$ in
Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). Seed replication therefore varies the model, not the
evaluation window, and all reported variability is training variability. Every
number in this paper must be read in that light; see
Sections~\ref{sec:validity} and~\ref{sec:missing}.

\subsection{Baselines and configurations}
All heads sit on an otherwise identical model. The comparison is generated by a
single override, \texttt{model.head=\{factor\_model, normalizing\_flow,
copula\_spline\}}, over the same configuration file, so dataset, split, features,
architecture widths, optimiser (Adam, \citealp{kingma2015adam}), learning rate,
schedule, batch size, early-stopping rule, Monte Carlo sample counts and scoring
procedure are shared by construction. Seeds $0..11$ are used for every
(dataset, head) cell, giving matched pairs.

The repository also contains three further heads --- a skew-$t$ with an
Azzalini-type representation \citep{azzalini2003skewt}, a parameter-tied skew-$t$,
and a Gaussian mixture --- and a spline-then-flow composition. Results for these
exist only as overall scores on five seeds and are reported separately rather than
mixed into the main table.

\subsection{Metrics and testing}
We report Equation~\eqref{eq:scrps} per level and overall, with the quantile-integral
CRPS estimator on a $1\%$ grid and $1000$ predictive samples. Because seeds are
matched across heads, we test each candidate against the Gaussian baseline with a
two-sided \emph{paired} Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the twelve seeds, and report
the win count. The test asks whether the candidate beats the baseline for a typical
seed on this single test window; it is not a test over datasets, over forecast
origins, or over resamplings of the data.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Overall accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:overall} is the main result. The flow head lowers overall sCRPS on
five of the six datasets for which it was run, with paired $p < 0.05$ on labour,
prison and tourism-small, and $p = 0.052$ on tourism-large. It raises sCRPS on
traffic by $5.9\%$, which is not significant. The copula-spline head improves four
of seven, significantly on wiki2 and tourism-large.

The two heads do not succeed in the same places. The flow's largest gains (prison
$-7.3\%$, tourism-small $-6.6\%$) are datasets where the spline does nothing or
hurts; the spline's clearest gain (wiki2 $-3.0\%$, $p=0.012$) is a dataset where
the flow is flat ($-0.5\%$, $p=0.79$). Only tourism-large favours both. Since the
two differ solely in \emph{where} the learned marginal is applied, this is direct
evidence that placement, not flexibility, drives the outcome.

\subsection{Accuracy by aggregation level}
Table~\ref{tab:levels} breaks the flow's result down by level. Two patterns are
consistent. First, the gain is not confined to the bottom level: on prison the
largest improvements are at \emph{Total} ($-29.3\%$) and \emph{Gender} ($-28.3\%$),
both aggregates, while the bottom level is $2.5\%$ \emph{worse}. Second, on the
datasets where the flow loses, it loses at the top: traffic's Total worsens by
$20.7\%$ and Halves by $21.9\%$ while its bottom level (Lanes) is unchanged
($-0.5\%$).

This is what Equation~\eqref{eq:coherent} predicts. Aggregates are sums of clipped
bottom draws, so their distributions depend on the joint law, not only the marginals.
A change to the marginal shape propagates upward and is amplified by summation over
many series. It follows that a marginal-only intervention should not be evaluated at
the bottom level alone --- and, less comfortably, that improvements at aggregate
levels may reflect an incidentally better-calibrated sum rather than a better
marginal. We did not measure which.

\subsection{Is the data non-Gaussian, and does it matter?}
\label{sec:nongauss}

The motivation for the method is an empirical claim about the data, and the claim had
never been tested. We test it with a deliberately model-free proxy. For each series
we form seasonal-naive residuals $e_t = y_t - y_{t-m}$ at the period $m$ implied by
the observation frequency, standardise by the residual standard deviation, and apply
the D'Agostino--Pearson omnibus normality test \citep{dagostino1973}, controlling the
false-discovery rate across series at $5\%$ \citep{benjamini1995fdr}.

\paragraph{The proxy is a proxy.} Seasonal-naive residuals are not CLOVER's
conditional residuals. They remove level and seasonality without fitting anything ---
which is precisely why they cannot smuggle a distributional assumption into the
diagnostic --- but any remaining trend or heteroskedasticity inflates the apparent
departure from normality, and the power of the test scales with series length. Read
Table~\ref{tab:nongauss} as evidence about the plausibility of a fixed Gaussian
marginal, not as a measurement of the model's own residuals.

Three findings follow, and the second and third are negative.

\paragraph{(i) The Gaussian marginal is a poor description of most of these series.}
Normality is rejected for $95.7\%$ of tourism-large's and $100\%$ of traffic's and
wiki2's bottom series. The dominant departure is \emph{tail weight}, not skew: median
excess kurtosis reaches $49.9$ on wiki2 and $8.5$ on traffic while median skewness
is within $\pm 0.26$ everywhere.

\paragraph{(ii) The right-skew motivation is not supported for the tourism data.}
A common motivating claim --- that tourism series are right-skewed --- does not
survive this diagnostic. Median residual skewness is $-0.055$ on tourism-small and
$-0.007$ on tourism-large, with essentially half the series on each side. The
datasets with a detectable positive skew are prison (median $+0.258$, $71.9\%$
positive) and wiki2 (median $+0.200$, $70.7\%$ positive). Any skew-based motivation
in the paper must be attached to those two datasets, not to tourism.

\paragraph{(iii) Measured non-Gaussianity predicts the flow's benefit with the wrong
sign.} Across the six datasets with a flow run, the Spearman rank correlation between
bottom-level median excess kurtosis and the flow's overall $\Delta$ is
$\rho = +0.886$ ($p = 0.019$), and between rejection rate and $\Delta$ it is
$\rho = +0.870$ ($p = 0.024$). Since $\Delta < 0$ means the flow wins, a positive
correlation means the flow helps \emph{least} where the Gaussian assumption is most
violated. The two datasets where it helps most, prison and tourism-small, have the
\emph{weakest} measured departure from normality, and the dataset where it loses,
traffic, has among the strongest. For the copula-spline head the correlation is weak
and of the opposite sign ($\rho = -0.357$, $p = 0.432$, $n = 7$), i.e.\ in the
hypothesised direction but not distinguishable from noise.

\paragraph{Confounds we cannot separate.} With seven datasets this analysis is
fragile, and three confounds run alongside the effect. (a) The rejection rate is
partly a power artefact: tourism-small and prison have only 32 and 44 residuals per
series, so their low rejection rates partly reflect a weak test, not a Gaussian
truth. (b) Series length itself correlates with the flow's $\Delta$
($\rho = +0.714$, $p = 0.111$), and the datasets with heavy tails are the long ones.
(c) Hierarchy size correlates too ($\rho = +0.464$ for $\Nb$): the flow wins on the
small hierarchies (prison $\Nb{=}32$, tourism-small $\Nb{=}56$) and loses on the
large ones (traffic $\Nb{=}200$). A parameter-count explanation is therefore equally
consistent with Table~\ref{tab:nongauss}: the flow fits $3K_f = 24$ extra shape
parameters per $(\text{series}, \text{horizon})$, and the datasets where it fails are
those with the most outputs to fit them for. Median excess kurtosis is the strongest
single correlate, but $n=6$ cannot adjudicate between it and these alternatives.

\section{Discussion}

The headline is mixed and dataset-dependent, and we think the interesting content is
in \emph{why}.

A learned marginal is not a free improvement. It buys shape at the cost of
$3K_f$ parameters per series per horizon step, estimated from as few as 17 training
windows (prison, tourism-small). That the two datasets with the fewest windows show
the largest gains is, at first sight, paradoxical --- until one notes that they also
have the smallest hierarchies and hence the fewest outputs to fit shape parameters
for, and that with $h \cdot \Nb$ in the hundreds rather than the thousands the
effective sample per shape parameter is not as small as the window count suggests.

The placement result is the most portable finding. The flow warps the idiosyncratic
component and lets an untouched Gaussian factor term smooth the marginal back toward
normality (Equation~\eqref{eq:convolution}); the spline warps the whole correlated
draw and preserves rank correlation exactly. These are not two implementations of one
idea but two different models, and they win on disjoint datasets. Anyone adding a
learned marginal to a factor-structured forecaster faces this choice, and our
evidence is that it matters more than the choice of transform family.

The negative finding about motivation deserves emphasis. We built the diagnostic to
support the method's premise and it did the opposite: the measured departure from
normality is, if anything, an indicator that the flow will \emph{not} help. Three
readings are consistent with the data, and we cannot separate them at $n=6$. The
first is the convolution mechanism: heavy-tailed datasets here are the
high-dimensional ones, where cross-series structure and hence $\|\Fmat_i\|^2$ is
large, so $\rho_i$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:rho} is large and the flow's contribution to
the marginal is damped. The second is estimation: heavy-tailed data makes the shape
parameters harder to estimate, and per-output flexibility hurts most where estimates
are noisiest --- which the capacity ablation independently supports. The third is
that the correlation is an artefact of series length acting on both variables. We
regard the first two as the likely mechanisms and the required experiments to
distinguish them as the natural next step.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{No density, hence no likelihood.} The flow is used only forwards. This is
a deliberate consequence of scoring-rule training and it keeps sampling cheap, but it
means the model cannot report a predictive density, cannot be compared on
log-likelihood, and cannot be used for tasks needing $p(\bvec)$.

\paragraph{The marginal is not the flow's marginal.} By
Equation~\eqref{eq:convolution} the realised bottom-level marginal is a Gaussian
smoothing of the warped law. Describing the method as ``learning the marginal'' is
therefore imprecise for the primary head; it learns the marginal of one additive
component.

\paragraph{Parameters lose their meaning.} Once $T_\phi$ is non-affine, $\mu$ is not
a mean and $\sigma$ is not a standard deviation
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), and the parameterisation is redundant.

\paragraph{One test origin.} Every score comes from a single forecast creation date
per dataset. This is the protocol inherited from the repository's evaluation, and it
is the largest threat to every quantitative claim here.

\paragraph{Nothing about calibration or coherence was measured.} The repository's
calibration and PIT plotting path accepts only the Gaussian head and refuses the
others, so no calibration comparison exists. Coherence is exact by construction for
all heads and was never in question, but also never measured numerically. We
therefore make no claim about calibration, sharpness, interval coverage, or
coherence quality.

\paragraph{Cross-series dependence was not evaluated.} All reported metrics are
univariate CRPS aggregated over series. The repository implements an energy score but
no experiment used it, so we make no claim that any head represents cross-series
dependence better than another, even though the copula construction is designed to
preserve it.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:validity}

\paragraph{Single-origin test sets.} As above: $\text{test}=1$ everywhere.
Seed variance is training variance, not forecast-origin variance, and the two should
not be conflated.

\paragraph{Runs are not bit-reproducible across invocations.} Comparing the 65
(dataset, head, seed) cells that appear in two different benchmark invocations of the
same code, \emph{none} are identical: $27$ differ by more than $1\%$ relative and $8$
by more than $5\%$, the largest being traffic/Gaussian/seed~3 at $0.024311$ versus
$0.016233$ ($33\%$). Nothing between the two commits changes the Gaussian code path,
so this is run-to-run nondeterminism, not a version effect. The likely cause is
nondeterministic floating-point reduction order under differing CPU thread counts
(the benchmark sets \texttt{torch.set\_num\_threads} per worker), amplified by early
stopping selecting a different step and by the single-window test set
[NEEDS VERIFICATION]. \emph{Consequence:} the paired comparisons in
Table~\ref{tab:overall} remain internally valid, because both heads were run in the
same invocation under the same conditions, but no individual cell should be treated
as exactly reproducible, and traffic's per-seed spread exceeds the flow-versus-
Gaussian difference we report for it.

\paragraph{One direction of the traffic result flips between experiment versions.}
An earlier five-seed benchmark reports the flow \emph{beating} Gaussian on traffic by
$10.2\%$; the twelve-seed benchmark reports it losing by $5.9\%$, and losing by
$7.1\%$ on the first five seeds alone. We report the twelve-seed result and flag the
conflict rather than choosing the convenient number. Given the nondeterminism above,
the honest statement about traffic is that its result is not resolved.

\paragraph{Provenance of some result files.} The main twelve-seed benchmark has a
generating script in the repository. The five-seed sweep, the capacity sweep, the
tail-metric file and the copula sweeps do not: no committed script produces their
head labels (\texttt{n4}, \texttt{n16}) or their pinball metrics. Their numbers are
consistent with the configuration they claim, but they cannot be regenerated from the
repository as it stands. Tables~\ref{tab:capacity} and the previous subsection should
be read with that caveat; Tables~\ref{tab:overall}--\ref{tab:complexity} are
reproducible.

\paragraph{Unequal seed counts.} Police has 5 Gaussian seeds against 12 spline seeds.
Comparing those means directly gives $+0.52\%$; comparing on the 5 common seeds gives
$+1.29\%$. We report the paired figure.

\paragraph{No hyperparameter tuning per head.} Architecture widths, learning rate and
schedule were tuned (by whatever process produced the configuration files) for the
Gaussian head and reused unchanged. This is the fair-by-construction choice for
isolating the head, but it may disadvantage the candidates.

\paragraph{Multiplicity.} Table~\ref{tab:overall} performs 13 paired tests without a
family-wise correction. Under a Holm correction at $5\%$ only tourism-small/flow
($p=0.002$) and tourism-large/spline ($p=0.005$) would survive. We report uncorrected
$p$-values and state this explicitly rather than implying stronger evidence than
exists.

\section{Experiments that should be run before publication}
\label{sec:missing}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Rolling-origin evaluation.} Score several test origins per dataset
instead of one, and report variability over origins separately from variability over
seeds. Without this, no claim in this paper is safely generalisable. \emph{This is
the single most important missing experiment.}
\item \textbf{Complete the police cell.} Twelve seeds of the flow head on police, and
seeds 5--11 of the Gaussian baseline, so the largest hierarchy is in the main table.
\item \textbf{Measure $\rho_i$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:rho}} on trained models. This
directly tests the convolution explanation: if the flow fails where $\rho$ is large,
the mechanism is confirmed; if not, it is not the mechanism.
\item \textbf{Calibration and PIT for every head.} Extend the plotting path beyond
the Gaussian head, and report interval coverage and PIT histograms per level, so a
calibration claim becomes possible.
\item \textbf{Energy score.} Evaluate the joint objective already implemented, to test
whether the copula head's rank-correlation guarantee has a measurable benefit.
\item \textbf{Held-out capacity selection.} Choose $K_f$ on validation rather than
reporting a fixed default, and rerun the main table with the selected value.
\item \textbf{A matched-capacity control.} Widen the Gaussian head's MLP by the
flow's parameter count, so the comparison separates ``a learned marginal'' from
``more parameters''.
\item \textbf{Determinism.} Pin thread counts and enable deterministic kernels, then
re-verify that a seed reproduces exactly; re-run traffic under that regime.
\item \textbf{Regenerate the orphaned result files} from committed scripts, or drop
them from the paper.
\item \textbf{Skew-$t$ and mixture baselines at 12 seeds}, so the flow is compared
against other non-Gaussian families and not only against the Gaussian.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Hierarchical forecasting.} Reconciliation via optimal combination
\citep{hyndman2011optimal} and trace minimisation \citep{wickramasuriya2019optimal}
established the projection view. Probabilistic extensions reconcile distributions
rather than points \citep{taieb2017coherent} and formalise what probabilistic
coherence means and how to score it \citep{panagiotelis2023probabilistic}.
End-to-end neural approaches impose coherence inside the model
\citep{rangapuram2021endtoend}; CLOVER \citep{olivares2024clover} does so through a
Gaussian factor reparameterisation and is the model we modify.

\paragraph{Non-Gaussian and flow-based multivariate forecasting.} DeepAR
\citep{salinas2020deepar} popularised per-series parametric likelihoods; the low-rank
Gaussian copula process \citep{salinas2019copula} added learned marginals on top of a
low-rank Gaussian dependence structure --- the closest antecedent to our
copula-spline head, differing mainly in using an empirical rather than a learned
parametric marginal transform. Conditioned normalizing flows
\citep{rasul2021multivariate} model the multivariate predictive density directly.

\paragraph{Flows for hierarchical series specifically.} This combination already
exists in the literature. NeuralReconciler \citep{wang2024neuralreconciler} uses a
normalizing flow with attention-based trainable reconciliation, explicitly motivated
by non-Gaussian hierarchical distributions; a conditional-flow reconciliation layer on
top of an autoregressive transformer appears in
\citep{autoregressiveflowrecon2022}; and a flow-based reconciliation transformer
appears in \citep{frt2025}. Accordingly we make \textbf{no novelty claim} of the form
``first to apply normalizing flows to hierarchical forecasting''.

\paragraph{Positioning.} What distinguishes this work is the setting and the
question, not the ingredient. All three flow-based hierarchical systems above place
the flow in or around a \emph{reconciliation} step. We instead place a learned
marginal inside a model that is already coherent by construction, where no
reconciliation step exists, and ask whether the marginal family --- holding the
coherence mechanism, the dependence structure and the objective fixed --- is where
the remaining accuracy is. Our contribution is best classified as an
\textbf{integration and ablation contribution with a negative result about the
motivating premise}, not as a new flow architecture.

\section{Conclusion}

Replacing a coherent hierarchical forecaster's fixed Gaussian marginal with a
conditional deep sigmoidal flow lowered overall scaled CRPS on five of six datasets,
significantly on three, at a negligible parameter cost and a modest training cost,
while leaving hierarchical coherence exactly intact by construction. It raised sCRPS
on one dataset. A second placement of the same idea, warping the whole correlated
draw so that rank correlation is preserved, improved a different and largely disjoint
set of datasets, which suggests that where a learned marginal is inserted matters
more than which transform family is used.

The motivating hypothesis did not survive contact with the data. Bottom-level
residuals depart sharply from normality on most of these datasets --- through tail
weight rather than skew --- but that departure is associated with the flow helping
\emph{less}, not more. The implementation offers a candidate explanation, since the
primary head's additive Gaussian factor term convolves the learned shape back toward
normality at a rate set by its variance share, and the capacity ablation offers a
second in the form of an estimation-variance cost that grows with the number of
outputs. Distinguishing them, and repeating all of this over multiple forecast
origins rather than one, is what the result needs before it can be called settled.
